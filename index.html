Chapter 0 · History

The Greatest Untold Story of Our Time

Group A

Before Christopher Columbus set foot in the Americas in 1492, the continents were home to hundreds of millions of people. Scholars estimate that between 50 and 100 million indigenous people lived in North, Central, and South America — more than the entire population of Europe at that time. They spoke over 2,000 different languages and had built complex cities, trade networks, and democratic systems of government that would later influence the founders of the United States.

Yet most school textbooks around the world devote only a few pages to these civilisations. The story of Native Americans — their achievements, their suffering, and their survival — is rarely told in full. This is why many scholars call it "the greatest untold story of our time." Within 150 years of European contact, up to 90 percent of the Indigenous population had died from disease, warfare, and forced displacement. Entire languages, traditions, and knowledge systems vanished almost overnight.

Understanding this history is not just about the past. Its consequences — poverty, land loss, and cultural erasure — shape the lives of the roughly 9.7 million Native Americans living in the United States today. To understand the present, we must first reckon honestly with what was lost and why it has so long been ignored.

Chapter 1

Educating a Non-Native Audience on Native Americans Today

Group B

Many people outside — and even inside — the United States hold deeply inaccurate images of Native Americans. Films, sports mascots, and Halloween costumes have created a cartoon version of indigenous people: feathered, silent, and stuck in the past. This stereotype is not only wrong — it is harmful.

Today, Native Americans are doctors, artists, engineers, activists, and teachers. They live in cities and on reservations, in Alaska and in Florida. They are a living, diverse population of nearly 10 million people, yet they remain largely invisible in mainstream media. When Native voices are missing from public conversations, policy decisions are made without their input — affecting everything from healthcare to land rights.

Education is the most powerful tool for change. When non-native students, politicians, and citizens learn about the real history and contemporary reality of Native Americans, they become better equipped to challenge discrimination and support indigenous rights. Museums, community programmes, and school curricula that include accurate Native perspectives help to break down prejudice. Listening to Native voices directly — through books, films, and podcasts made by indigenous people themselves — is an essential first step toward genuine understanding and respect.

Chapter 2

Understanding Historical Trauma for Native American Families

Group B Group C

Historical trauma refers to the deep emotional and psychological wounds that are passed down through generations as a result of large-scale tragedies. For Native Americans, centuries of colonisation, forced removals, and the brutal boarding school system created wounds that did not simply heal when those events ended. Children who experienced or witnessed trauma grew into parents who unknowingly passed on patterns of pain.

Researchers have described a cycle that tends to repeat itself in affected communities: unhealed grief leads to low self-esteem, which can lead to self-medication through alcohol or drugs, which in turn causes violence or neglect within families, and these behaviours become normalised — accepted as ordinary life — so the cycle continues into the next generation. Understanding this cycle is crucial: it is not a sign of weakness or deficiency in Native culture, but a predictable response to extreme, sustained suffering.

One important tool for healing is storytelling. When elders share traditional stories with young people, they pass on identity, values, and a sense of belonging that counters feelings of worthlessness. Storytelling reconnects communities to a time before the trauma began — a time of strength, wisdom, and dignity.

Chapter 3

Native American Family Life, Community, and the Importance of the Tribe

Group A

In most Native American cultures, family is not limited to parents and children. The extended family — grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins — plays a central role in raising children and supporting adults through difficult times. Elders are treated with profound respect because they carry the community's history and traditional knowledge. This web of relationships provides a social safety net that binds the community together.

The tribe — or nation — functions as a larger family. Tribal membership gives individuals a clear sense of identity: who they are, where they come from, and what values they hold. Ceremonies, festivals, and communal work strengthen these bonds across generations. Many Native groups make decisions through consensus — all voices are heard before a choice is made — a practice that reflects deep respect for every community member.

Colonial policies deliberately targeted these family structures. Children were removed to boarding schools, traditional ceremonies were banned, and tribal governance was undermined. The survival of strong family and community ties despite these attacks is one of the most remarkable stories of resilience in human history. Understanding the centrality of family and tribe helps explain why protecting tribal sovereignty and cultural continuity matters so profoundly to Native peoples.

Chapter 4

The Revitalization of Native American Life and Culture

Group C

Across the United States and Canada, Native communities are reclaiming what was nearly taken from them. This process — known as cultural revitalization — takes many forms. Language immersion schools teach children entirely in their ancestral tongue, reversing decades of language loss. In some communities, the number of fluent speakers of languages like Cherokee, Lakota, and Diné (Navajo) is actually growing for the first time in over a century.

Cultural centres and museums run by Native people themselves tell indigenous history from the inside. Annual pow-wows bring thousands of people together for drumming, dance, and ceremony — events that celebrate identity and strengthen community bonds. Traditional crafts, music, and healing practices are being documented and taught to younger generations before the last elders who remember them pass on.

Digital technology has become an unlikely ally. Native youth are creating YouTube channels, podcasts, and social-media accounts to share their languages and stories with the world. Organisations such as the First Nations Development Institute and the American Indian College Fund support Native students financially and culturally, helping them succeed in higher education without abandoning their roots. Revitalization is not nostalgia — it is an active, creative movement building a stronger future.

Chapter 5

Tribal Land and the Struggle for Native American Property Rights

Group B

Land is not merely property to Native Americans — it is identity, spirituality, and survival. Yet since the arrival of European colonisers, indigenous peoples have lost approximately 98 percent of the land that was once theirs. Treaties — legal agreements signed between Native nations and the US government — promised tribes specific territories in exchange for peace. Once they became inconvenient, however, most of these treaties were broken.

Today, Native land issues remain deeply contested. Reservations are often located on land that was considered worthless at the time they were created — only for oil, minerals, or water to be discovered beneath them later. Energy companies, governments, and private developers have repeatedly sought to drill, mine, or build pipelines across tribal territories without meaningful consent. The 2016 Standing Rock protests, where thousands gathered to oppose an oil pipeline threatening the water supply of the Sioux Nation, brought these struggles into global view.

Legal battles over treaty rights continue in courts today. Many tribes face challenges to their fishing rights, hunting rights, and the right to govern activities on their own land. Limited resources make it difficult to fight long legal battles against well-funded opponents. Land, for Native Americans, is the foundation of everything — and its protection remains one of the most urgent struggles of the present day.

Chapter 6

Native American Languages: Voices on the Verge of Silence

All Groups

When a language dies, an entire way of seeing the world disappears with it. Before European colonisation, more than 300 Native American languages were spoken in what is now the United States. Today, fewer than 150 remain, and most of those are spoken by only a handful of elderly people. Experts predict that without urgent action, the majority could vanish within a generation.

Language loss was not accidental. The US government actively promoted policies designed to destroy Native languages. In boarding schools, children were physically punished for speaking their mother tongue — a practice intended to assimilate them into white American society. The shame and silence that resulted scarred communities for decades.

Now, recovery efforts are under way. The Māori model from New Zealand — where language nests (small immersion schools for toddlers) helped revive the Māori language — has inspired Native American communities from Hawaii to Montana. In 2006, the Myaamia tribe of Indiana partnered with Miami University to document and teach their language. In just two decades, the number of speakers has grown from near zero. Language revitalization is slow, difficult work — but it is also an act of profound sovereignty: reclaiming the very words your community thinks in.

Chapter 7

Cultural Disruption: The Impact of Colonization on Native Americans

Group A

The word colonisation describes more than a political event — it describes the systematic dismantling of a people's entire world. When European powers claimed the Americas, they did not simply take land; they destroyed social structures, outlawed spiritual practices, separated children from families, and erased centuries of accumulated knowledge. The impact was catastrophic and continues to reverberate across generations.

Perhaps no policy illustrates this destruction more clearly than the Indian boarding school system. Between 1869 and the 1970s, more than 150,000 Native children were forcibly removed from their homes and sent to government-run or church-run schools. The stated goal was stark: "Kill the Indian, save the man." Children arrived to find their hair cut, their clothes replaced, and their languages forbidden. Those who survived often returned to their communities as strangers — unable to speak their own language or participate in their own ceremonies.

The consequences of this deliberate cultural disruption can be measured today in higher rates of poverty, mental illness, and substance abuse on reservations. However, understanding the cause — colonisation — also points the way toward solutions: restoring land, funding cultural programmes, and allowing tribes to govern themselves once more.

Chapter 8

Our Future – Looking Forward with Hope

Group C

Despite centuries of loss and struggle, Native American communities are not defined by suffering alone — they are defined by extraordinary resilience and a determined vision for the future. A new generation of indigenous leaders, artists, scientists, and activists is rising, one that refuses to choose between being Native and being modern.

Several organisations play a vital role in supporting this journey. The American Indian College Fund provides scholarships and mentoring to Native students in higher education. The First Nations Development Institute helps tribes build economic strength on their own terms. Youth-led movements such as Rezpect Our Water — which started when teenagers from the Standing Rock Sioux reservation protested the Dakota Access Pipeline — have shown the world that Native youth are powerful advocates for their communities and for the planet.

Storytelling remains a cornerstone of this hope. When indigenous writers, filmmakers, and musicians share their stories, they challenge stereotypes and inspire both native and non-native audiences. Authors like Tommy Orange (Cheyenne and Arapaho) and filmmakers like Sterlin Harjo (Seminole/Muscogee) are reaching global audiences with honest, complex, and deeply human portraits of Native life. The future holds challenges — but it also holds voices, stories, and dreams that deserve to be heard.
